Stamp Duty Land Tax 2026: what you'll actually pay

Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) is one of the biggest one-off costs in a property purchase, and one of the most commonly misunderstood — mostly because people assume it's a flat percentage of the whole purchase price. It isn't.

How SDLT is actually banded

SDLT works like Income Tax: it's charged in slices, not as one flat rate on the full price. A 0% band covers the first slice of the purchase price, then progressively higher rates apply only to the portion of the price that falls within each subsequent band — not retroactively to the whole amount. This is why a small increase in purchase price rarely causes a dramatic jump in the tax owed; only the portion above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate.

First-time buyer relief

First-time buyers — people who have never owned a residential property anywhere in the world — typically benefit from a higher 0% threshold than home movers, up to a price cap above which the relief stops applying entirely and standard rates take over. This relief only applies to a main residence, not a buy-to-let or second property.

The additional property surcharge

Buying an additional residential property — a second home or buy-to-let — usually adds a surcharge on top of the standard SDLT bands, applied across the whole price rather than just the portion above a threshold. This surcharge is a major reason buy-to-let and second-home purchase costs have risen sharply in recent years, and it applies even if the additional property is relatively low-value.

SDLT only applies in England and Northern Ireland. Scotland has its own Land and Buildings Transaction Tax (LBTT), and Wales has Land Transaction Tax (LTT) — both with different bands and rules, so don't apply English SDLT figures to a Scottish or Welsh purchase.

When and how you pay it

SDLT is normally due within a set window after completion (currently 14 days), and in practice it's almost always handled by your solicitor or conveyancer as part of the completion process, who files the return and payment on your behalf. Getting the figure wrong at the offer stage, though, is a common budgeting mistake — it's worth calculating it properly before you make an offer, not after.
