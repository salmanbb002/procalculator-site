Inch to mm: the essential conversion for engineering, DIY and fixings

Millimetres are the standard unit for precision measurement on UK engineering drawings, tools and most modern components — but a huge amount of hardware still uses imperial (inch and fractional-inch) sizing, especially fasteners, plumbing fittings, older machinery, US-manufactured parts and specific trades like woodworking. That mix is exactly why "inch to mm" is one of the most consistently searched conversions among tradespeople, engineers and DIY enthusiasts, not just casual users.

One inch equals exactly 25.4mm — a fixed, internationally agreed figure with zero approximation, so an inch-to-mm conversion is always precise arithmetic. This calculator handles both directions: inches into millimetres, for reading an imperial spec (from a drill bit, bolt, or US component) in the metric figure needed for a UK tool or drawing, and millimetres back into inches for the reverse.

Conversion formula and method

The inch-to-mm relationship is a single fixed multiplication:

Millimetres = Inches × 25.4
Inches = Millimetres ÷ 25.4

Worked example: inches to mm

Take 5/8 inch, a common socket size. Convert the fraction to a decimal first: 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625. Multiply by 25.4: 0.625 × 25.4 = 15.875mm. So 5/8" converts to 15.875mm, usually rounded to 15.88mm.

Worked example: mm to inches

Take 10mm, a common metric bolt size. Divide by 25.4: 10 ÷ 25.4 = 0.3937in. This is the calculation the "MM → Inches" mode above runs automatically, and the calculator also rounds to the nearest 1/16" for a practical ruler reading.

Converting a mixed fraction

For a measurement like 2 3/8 inches, convert the fraction first (3 ÷ 8 = 0.375), add it to the whole number (2.375in), then multiply by 25.4: 2.375 × 25.4 = 60.325mm.

Where this conversion actually gets used

  • Fasteners and fixings: UK hardware stores sell both metric (M6, M8, M10...) and imperial (1/4", 5/16", 3/8"...) bolts, nuts and drill bits — telling them apart and cross-referencing sizes requires this exact conversion.
  • Plumbing: UK plumbing fittings are a well-known mix of metric pipe and older imperial fitting sizes (1/2", 3/4", 1" BSP threads are still standard), making inch-to-mm conversion a routine trade task.
  • Woodworking and US-sourced plans: American woodworking plans and lumber dimensions are specified in inches, which UK woodworkers using metric tools and timber need to convert.
  • Engineering and manufacturing: imported components, especially from the US, are frequently specified in inches and fractional inches, requiring conversion to match metric drawings and tolerances.
  • Screen and display sizing: screen sizes are conventionally quoted in inches worldwide, but bezel, mounting and cable specifications are often in millimetres.

Reading a tape measure: understanding the fractional markings

Standard UK and US tape measures mark inches in a repeating binary sequence — halves, then quarters, then eighths, then sixteenths — rather than decimals, which is why the fraction-to-mm table above is arranged in sixteenths rather than a decimal step. The longer lines on a tape measure mark whole inches and halves; progressively shorter lines mark quarters, eighths and finally sixteenths. Recognising this pattern makes it much faster to read off a fractional measurement directly, without needing to count every tick mark from zero.

Historical context: two systems bridged by a single fixed number

The inch has ancient origins, historically estimated from body proportions such as the width of a thumb, and varied slightly between countries and eras before formal standardisation. The millimetre is a purely modern metric unit — one-thousandth of a metre — introduced when the metric system was formalised in France in the 1790s and later adopted internationally through the Metre Convention of 1875. For most of their shared history, the two units had no single agreed exact relationship, meaning conversions were approximate and varied slightly by country.

That changed in 1959 with the International Yard and Pound Agreement, signed by the UK, US, Australia, Canada, New Zealand and South Africa, which fixed the inch at exactly 25.4mm. This single, deliberately chosen bridge value is why every inch-to-mm conversion today, in every English-speaking country, uses precisely the same factor — a level of international consistency that didn't exist before 1959, when UK and US inches could differ by tiny but real fractions of a millimetre.

Common mistakes when converting inches to mm

  • Misreading a fraction's denominator. 3/16" and 3/8" look superficially similar written down but convert to very different millimetre values (4.76mm vs 9.53mm) — always double-check the denominator before converting or ordering a part.
  • Rounding a metric-imperial near-match too casually. 10mm and 3/8" (9.525mm) are close but not identical — using one bolt or socket size in place of the other can strip threads or fail to fit properly, even though the numbers look close.
  • Forgetting that "mm to nearest sixteenth" is an approximation. When a calculator rounds a millimetre figure to the nearest 1/16" for ruler-reading convenience, that's a practical rounding for marking a cut — not the exact converted value, which should be used for anything precision-critical.
  • Confusing metric (M) bolt sizes with millimetre diameter directly. An M8 bolt has an 8mm nominal shaft diameter, but thread pitch and head size vary by standard — the "M" number isn't a simple, complete substitute for a full spec when ordering imperial-metric equivalents.

Accuracy and precision note

Because 1 inch = 25.4mm is a fixed, internationally agreed definition rather than a measured approximation, every conversion on this page and in the calculator above is mathematically exact. Millimetre figures are shown to two decimal places above for the precision that engineering and fastener work typically needs — full floating-point precision is available in the calculator for anything requiring an even finer figure.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert inches to mm?

Multiply the inch figure by 25.4. For example, 1 inch × 25.4 = 25.4mm exactly. This is a fixed, internationally defined relationship with no approximation involved.

What is 1 inch in mm?

1 inch is exactly 25.4mm — the base conversion figure that every other inch-to-mm conversion is built from.

How do I convert mm to inches?

Divide the millimetre figure by 25.4. For example, 10mm ÷ 25.4 = 0.394in.

What is 1/4 inch in mm?

1/4 inch is 6.35mm — a very commonly needed conversion for drill bits, bolts and fittings sold in fractional-inch sizes.

What is 1/2 inch in mm?

1/2 inch is exactly 12.7mm.

How many mm is 3/8 inch?

3/8 inch is 9.525mm, commonly rounded to 9.5mm for socket and spanner sizing.

Why is 1 inch exactly 25.4mm?

Because the inch and millimetre come from independent measurement systems that were formally reconciled in 1959, when the international yard and pound agreement fixed the inch at exactly 25.4mm — a deliberately chosen exact bridge value rather than a naturally round number in either system.

How accurate is an inch to mm conversion?

It's exact, not approximate — 1 inch = 25.4mm is a fixed, legally defined relationship, so every conversion is precise arithmetic. Any rounding you see is only in the number of decimal places displayed.

What is 5/16 inch in mm?

5/16 inch is 7.9375mm, commonly rounded to 7.94mm — a size that comes up often in socket sets and imperial fastener charts.

Can I use an M8 bolt instead of a 5/16 inch bolt?

Not reliably. An M8 bolt (8mm) and a 5/16in bolt (7.94mm) are close in diameter but have different thread pitches and tolerances — they should not be treated as interchangeable, even though the size difference looks negligible on paper.

What is 1 3/4 inches in mm?

1 3/4 inches is 44.45mm — convert the fraction (3÷4=0.75), add it to the whole number (1.75in), then multiply by 25.4.

A quick note

All conversions above use the exact internationally defined relationship between the inch and the millimetre. For anything precision-critical (engineering, structural or commercial use), always confirm against the source figures rather than a rounded display value.
