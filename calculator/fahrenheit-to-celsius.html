Fahrenheit to Celsius: bridging the UK's weather scale and the US's

Celsius is the UK's official temperature scale — weather forecasts, oven settings, medical thermometers and scientific work all use it — but Fahrenheit still turns up constantly in US media, US-manufactured appliances, older UK generations' everyday speech, and American recipes and weather reports. Unlike most metric-imperial conversions, Fahrenheit and Celsius aren't related by a simple multiplication: the relationship is a linear formula involving both a scaling factor and an offset, because the two scales don't share a common zero point.

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit originally set 0°F near the freezing point of a brine solution and 96°F near human body temperature, while Anders Celsius (in its modern form) fixed 0°C at water's freezing point and 100°C at its boiling point — two entirely different reference systems that happen to convert exactly, just not by a single clean multiplier. This calculator handles the conversion both ways instantly, without needing to remember the formula.

Conversion formula and method

The Fahrenheit-Celsius relationship is a fixed linear formula, exact to as many decimal places as needed:

Celsius = (Fahrenheit − 32) × 5⁄9
Fahrenheit = (Celsius × 9⁄5) + 32

Worked example: Fahrenheit to Celsius

Take 75°F, a common "warm day" reading. Subtract 32: 75 − 32 = 43. Multiply by 5/9: 43 × 5/9 = 23.9°C. So 75°F converts to 23.9°C.

Worked example: Celsius to Fahrenheit

Take 25°C, a warm UK summer day. Multiply by 9/5: 25 × 9/5 = 45. Add 32: 45 + 32 = 77°F. This is the calculation the "°C → °F" mode above runs automatically.

The −40 coincidence

There's exactly one temperature where both scales agree: −40. Plugging −40 into either formula returns −40 on the other scale — the only point where the Fahrenheit and Celsius number lines cross, a quirk of the specific slope and offset in the conversion formula rather than any deliberate design.

Temperature in context: real-world reference points

  • 0°C / 32°F — the freezing point of water, and the temperature UK weather forecasts flag as an ice/frost warning threshold.
  • 20°C / 68°F — a widely recommended comfortable indoor living-room temperature in UK homes and offices.
  • 37°C / 98.6°F — average human body temperature, the reference point most UK and US thermometers are calibrated around.
  • 25°C / 77°F — commonly regarded as a warm, pleasant UK summer day, well above the seasonal average.
  • 100°C / 212°F — the boiling point of water at standard sea-level atmospheric pressure, and a reference point for cooking and sterilisation.

Historical context: two scales, two inventors, one late reconciliation

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit devised his scale around 1724, initially anchoring it to the freezing point of a salt-and-ice brine mixture (0°F) and an estimate of human body temperature (originally 96°F, later refined). It became the dominant scale across the English-speaking world for the following two centuries. Anders Celsius proposed his scale in 1742, originally the reverse of today's version (0° at boiling, 100° at freezing) before being flipped to its now-familiar orientation shortly after his death.

Celsius (long called "centigrade" until 1948) became the international scientific standard and was adopted by most of the world during their various shifts to the metric system. The UK adopted Celsius for weather forecasting and most public use during its metrication programme from the 1960s onward, formally switching official weather broadcasts in 1962 — though older generations and some contexts kept using Fahrenheit informally for years afterward. The US never metricated and remains one of the only major economies still using Fahrenheit as its everyday public standard.

Where Fahrenheit to Celsius conversion actually gets used

  • Cooking with US recipes: American recipes and oven dials are in Fahrenheit, while UK ovens are marked in Celsius (and often gas mark) — a frequent source of "what temperature do I actually need" searches.
  • US weather and travel: UK travellers checking US weather forecasts, or reading US news, need to translate Fahrenheit readings into a Celsius figure they can intuitively judge.
  • Older appliances and thermometers: some imported or vintage equipment is calibrated in Fahrenheit, requiring conversion for UK use.
  • Medicine and science: body temperature is sometimes quoted in Fahrenheit in US medical contexts or older UK sources, needing conversion for comparison against Celsius-based NHS guidance.
  • Home brewing and fermentation: US brewing and fermentation guides often specify Fahrenheit temperature ranges for yeast activity, which UK hobbyists need translated to match their Celsius-only thermometers and fridges.
  • 3D printing and manufacturing: nozzle and bed temperature settings on some printers and machinery, especially US-designed hardware, default to Fahrenheit and need converting for UK-sourced filament and material spec sheets written in Celsius.

Because temperature conversion involves an offset rather than a simple multiplier, it's one of the few unit conversions genuinely difficult to do reliably in your head — which is exactly why a quick calculator earns its place here more than for most other conversions on this site.

Common mistakes when converting Fahrenheit to Celsius

  • Forgetting to subtract 32 before multiplying. The most common error is applying the ×5/9 factor directly to the raw Fahrenheit figure without subtracting 32 first — this produces a badly wrong result, since the two scales don't share a zero point.
  • Using the wrong fraction direction. Fahrenheit to Celsius uses 5/9; Celsius to Fahrenheit uses 9/5 — mixing these up is a very easy slip that produces a plausible-looking but incorrect answer.
  • Assuming a simple linear scale-up works. Unlike metric-imperial length or weight conversions, temperature has no fixed multiplier alone — the offset (32, or −32) is not optional and must always be included.
  • Confusing a Celsius interval with a Celsius reading. A "10-degree rise" in Fahrenheit is not a 10-degree rise in Celsius — a temperature *change* of 1°C equals a change of 1.8°F, a different calculation from converting an absolute reading.

Accuracy and precision note

Because the Fahrenheit-Celsius relationship is a fixed, exactly defined linear formula, every conversion on this page and in the calculator above is mathematically exact — there's no measured approximation involved. Celsius and Fahrenheit figures above are shown to one decimal place for everyday readability; the underlying calculation carries full floating-point precision if a more exact figure is needed for scientific or engineering use.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert Fahrenheit to Celsius?

Subtract 32, then multiply by 5/9. For example, (98.6 − 32) × 5/9 = 66.6 × 5/9 = 37°C exactly. This is the standard normal human body temperature in both scales.

What is 98.6°F in Celsius?

98.6°F is exactly 37°C — the traditionally cited average human body temperature.

How do I convert Celsius to Fahrenheit?

Multiply by 9/5, then add 32. For example, 20°C × 9/5 + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68°F, a common comfortable room-temperature figure.

What is 20°C in Fahrenheit?

20°C is 68°F — a widely used comfortable indoor room-temperature reference in the UK.

What temperature is the same in both Celsius and Fahrenheit?

−40 degrees. −40°C and −40°F are the exact same temperature — the only point on both scales where the numbers coincide, because the formula (F−32)×5/9=C and F=C×9/5+32 intersect precisely at −40.

What is 0°C in Fahrenheit?

0°C — the freezing point of water — is 32°F.

What is 100°C in Fahrenheit?

100°C — the boiling point of water at sea level — is 212°F.

Why does the UK use Celsius but the US use Fahrenheit?

The UK officially switched to Celsius as part of its broader metrication programme from the 1960s onward, and weather, cooking and medical temperatures are now reported in Celsius. The US never formally metricated and has continued using Fahrenheit, which was the dominant scale across the English-speaking world before Celsius (originally "centigrade") became the international scientific and everyday standard almost everywhere else.

How accurate is a Fahrenheit to Celsius conversion?

It's exact, not approximate — the relationship between the two scales is a fixed linear formula with no measured approximation involved, so every conversion is precise arithmetic. Any rounding you see is only in the number of decimal places displayed.

What is 350°F in Celsius for baking?

350°F is 180°C in a conventional oven, or about 160°C in a fan oven — UK gas mark 4, one of the most common baking temperatures for cakes and general baking.

What is 40°C fever in Fahrenheit?

40°C is 104°F — a high fever that the NHS advises should prompt medical attention, well above the normal 37°C (98.6°F) baseline.

A quick note

All conversions above use the exact linear relationship between the Fahrenheit and Celsius scales. For anything precision-critical (medical, scientific or commercial use), always confirm against the source figures rather than a rounded display value.
