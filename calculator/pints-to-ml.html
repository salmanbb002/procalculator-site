Pints to ml: why "a pint" means two different volumes

A pint is a unit of liquid volume, and it's one of the few imperial measures that survived UK metrication almost untouched — draught beer, cider and doorstep milk are still legally sold by the pint in Britain. But "a pint" isn't a single fixed volume worldwide: a UK/imperial pint is 568.26 millilitres, while a US liquid pint is 473.18 millilitres — around 20% smaller. Both are correct, in their own system; the confusion only happens when a figure crosses the Atlantic without saying which one it means.

This calculator lets you pick UK/imperial or US pint explicitly and shows the converted value in the other system alongside it, so you can see exactly how far apart the two really are — useful for reading US recipes and product volumes, checking home-brewing or bar-measure figures, or just settling an argument about how big a pint glass "should" be.

Conversion formula and method

The formulas, using the exact defined volumes:

UK/imperial pint: ml = pints × 568.26125
US liquid pint: ml = pints × 473.176473

Worked example: UK pint

For 3 UK pints: 3 × 568.26125 = 1,704.78ml, or 1.705 litres — roughly the size of three standard cans of a fizzy drink combined.

Worked example: US pint

For 3 US pints: 3 × 473.176473 = 1,419.53ml, or 1.420 litres. Note the same "3 pints" label produces a noticeably different real-world volume depending which system is meant.

In fluid ounces

A UK pint equals exactly 20 imperial fluid ounces; a US pint equals exactly 16 US fluid ounces. This is a second source of confusion, since imperial and US fluid ounces are themselves slightly different sizes (28.41ml vs 29.57ml) — the pint discrepancy and the fluid-ounce discrepancy compound rather than cancel out.

Real-world comparisons

  • A pint glass (568ml): the standard UK pub pint glass, legally required to hold a full imperial pint when filled to the brim or to a marked line.
  • A large UK milk carton (2 pints / 1,136ml): a common doorstep and supermarket size, still labelled in pints alongside its metric equivalent.
  • A standard soft drink can (330ml): a little over half a UK pint (58%) but close to 70% of a US pint.
  • A UK blood donation (approximately 470ml): often described casually as "about a pint" — and it's actually closer to a US pint (473ml) than a UK one (568ml).
  • A standard wine glass pour (125–175ml): roughly a quarter to a third of a UK pint.

Historical context: two gallons, two pints

Before 1824, Britain used several different gallon definitions depending on what was being measured — an ale gallon, a wine gallon, and a corn (dry goods) gallon all differed in size, which caused constant confusion in trade. The Weights and Measures Act 1824 swept this away and created a single imperial gallon, defined as the volume occupied by 10 pounds of distilled water — from which the imperial pint (one-eighth of a gallon) gets its still-used value of 568.26ml.

The United States had already gained independence by 1824 and never adopted Britain's reform. Instead, the US continued using the older English wine gallon (231 cubic inches, a pre-1824 measure), which is smaller than the new imperial gallon — and that's the entire reason the US pint, at 473.18ml, is smaller than the UK pint today. The two units share a name and a common ancestor, but have been mathematically different since 1824.

The UK's shift to metric from the 1960s onward removed the pint from most contexts, but draught beer and cider, and milk sold in returnable containers, were specifically exempted and remain legally saleable in pints to this day — one of a small number of imperial-unit carve-outs that survived UK and EU metrication rules.

Where the pint is still used today

  • Draught beer and cider: UK law requires draught beer and cider to be sold in imperial pints, halves, or thirds — it's one of the last mandatory imperial measures in British retail.
  • Doorstep and some supermarket milk: milk sold in returnable glass bottles, and often supermarket cartons, is still marketed by the pint (1, 2, 4 or 6 pints) alongside the metric litre figure.
  • Everyday speech: "going for a pint" is shorthand for a beer regardless of the actual glass or measure used, and "a pint of milk" remains the default way people describe that quantity.
  • Home brewing and cocktail measures: UK home-brewing kits and some bar-measure tools use pints as a working unit, so knowing the exact ml figure matters for recipe accuracy.

Spirits, by contrast, are not measured in pints or fractions of a pint in UK licensed premises — they're legally served in fixed metric measures (25ml or 35ml, chosen by the venue), which is a separate rule from the pint requirement for beer and cider. Wine sold by the glass is also measured in millilitres (typically 125ml, 175ml or 250ml) rather than any pint-based fraction, so "pint" as a legal serving unit in the UK is specifically a beer-and-cider rule, not a general drinks-measuring standard.

Common mistakes when converting pints to ml

  • Using the wrong pint for the wrong country. Applying a US recipe's pint figure to a UK measuring jug (or vice versa) introduces a 20% volume error — always check whether a source is British or American before treating "a pint" as a fixed number.
  • Assuming imperial and US fluid ounces match. Even within "fluid ounces," an imperial fl oz (28.41ml) and a US fl oz (29.57ml) are different sizes, and pint totals expressed in fluid ounces (20 imperial vs 16 US) compound the difference rather than cancelling it out.
  • Treating pub "glass sizes" as fixed volumes. Some venues serve smaller measures like a "schooner" or two-thirds pint informally, which aren't the same as a legally defined third, half or full pint — check the actual serving size rather than assuming a round fraction.
  • Forgetting the permitted headspace on a poured pint. UK law allows a small amount of head/foam on a served beer, so a "full" glass may contain slightly under 568ml of liquid while still being a legally compliant pint — this is a serving-standard detail, not a measurement error in the unit itself.

Accuracy and precision note

Both the UK/imperial pint (568.26125ml, derived from the 4.54609-litre imperial gallon) and the US pint (473.176473ml, derived from the 3.785411784-litre US gallon) are exact, legally defined values — this calculator uses the full-precision figures rather than the commonly rounded "568ml" and "473ml" shorthand, so results are accurate down to fractions of a millilitre for calculations that need that level of precision (dosing, brewing, lab work).

Frequently asked questions

How many ml in a pint?

A UK (imperial) pint is 568.26ml. A US pint is smaller, at 473.18ml. Which one you need depends on context — draught beer, cider and doorstep milk in the UK are measured in imperial pints; US recipes and US-published figures use the US pint.

Is a UK pint the same as a US pint?

No. A UK/imperial pint (568.26ml) is about 20% larger than a US liquid pint (473.18ml). They come from different historical gallon definitions, so figures quoted in US sources for "a pint" will consistently be smaller than the UK figure.

How many ml in half a pint?

Half a UK pint is 284.13ml. Half a US pint is 236.59ml. Both are commonly rounded to 284ml and 237ml respectively in everyday use.

What is 2 pints in ml?

2 UK pints is 1,136.5ml (about 1.14 litres). 2 US pints is 946.4ml (about 0.95 litres) — very close to 1 US quart, since a US quart is defined as exactly 2 US pints.

How many ml is 4 pints?

4 UK pints is 2,273ml (2.273 litres) — the size of a standard large UK milk carton. 4 US pints is 1,892.7ml (1.893 litres), equal to exactly 1 US half-gallon.

Why is a British pint bigger than an American pint?

Britain redefined its gallon (and therefore its pint) in 1824 based on the volume of 10 pounds of water, creating the imperial system. The US had already broken from Britain by then and kept using the older English wine gallon (231 cubic inches), which is smaller — so the US pint stayed smaller too, and the two systems have never since been reconciled.

Is a pint of beer in the UK always exactly 568ml?

By law, draught beer and cider sold in the UK must be served in a third, half, or full imperial pint (or multiples of a third) — a full pint is 568.26ml, and pubs are legally required to serve a minimum of 95% liquid in a lined or oversized "to the brim" glass, so a small amount of head/foam is permitted.

How many ml in a quarter pint?

A quarter of a UK pint is 142.07ml. A quarter of a US pint is 118.29ml.

What is 1.5 pints in ml?

1.5 UK pints is 852.4ml. 1.5 US pints is 709.8ml.

How many millilitres is a can of beer or soft drink?

UK cans are commonly 330ml or 440ml, both smaller than a full 568ml UK pint — a 330ml can is a little over half a pint (58%), and a 440ml can is about 77% of a pint.

A quick note

All conversions above use the exact legal definitions of the imperial and US pint. For anything precision-critical (medical, legal, brewing or commercial use), always confirm against the source figures rather than a rounded display value.
