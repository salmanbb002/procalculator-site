Stone to kg: converting the UK's spoken weight unit into the metric standard

Stone is how most people in the UK and Ireland say their own body weight out loud, but kilograms are what appears on medical records, official documents and most modern scales — the exact reverse gap covered by our KG to Stone calculator. This page handles the same relationship starting from the other direction: taking a stone-and-pounds figure (like "11 stone 11 pounds") and converting it straight into kilograms, plus the reverse for anyone starting from a kilogram figure.

One stone equals exactly 14 pounds, and one pound equals exactly 0.45359237kg — both fixed, non-approximate values — so a stone-to-kg conversion is always precise arithmetic carried out in two linked steps. This calculator does both steps at once, and also handles the reverse direction, converting a kilogram figure back into the stone-and-pounds format.

Conversion formula and method

Converting stone to kilograms is a two-step calculation:

Pounds = (Stone × 14) + extra pounds
Kilograms = Pounds × 0.45359237

Worked example: stone and pounds to kg

Take 13 stone 5lb. Convert to total pounds: (13 × 14) + 5 = 187lb. Multiply by 0.45359237: 187 × 0.45359237 = 84.82kg. So 13st 5lb converts to 84.82kg.

Worked example: kg to stone and pounds

Take 75kg. Divide by 0.45359237: 75 ÷ 0.45359237 = 165.3lb. Divide by 14: 165.3 ÷ 14 = 11.81, so the stone figure is 11, and the remaining pounds are 165.3 − (11 × 14) = 11.3lb. Result: 75kg is 11 stone 11.3lb. This is the calculation the "KG → Stone + lb" mode above runs automatically.

A whole-stone shortcut

For a quick estimate with no extra pounds involved, multiply the stone figure by 6.35 (rather than the exact 6.35029) to get an approximate kilogram figure — accurate enough for a rough sense of weight, though always use the exact two-step method above for anything precise.

Weight in context: real-world reference points

  • 8 stone (50.8kg) — towards the lower end of the healthy weight range for an average adult female height, and a traditional featherweight boxing reference.
  • 10 stone (63.5kg) — around the midpoint of the healthy BMI range for an average UK adult height, and a frequently cited target weight in fitness contexts.
  • 12st 7lb (79.4kg) — close to the UK's cruiserweight boxing limit, and a commonly quoted "average adult male" reference figure.
  • 14 stone (88.9kg) — around the light-heavyweight to cruiserweight boundary in professional boxing.
  • 20 stone (127kg) — a figure that appears often in NHS weight-management contexts, roughly double the 10-stone reference point.

These are illustrative reference points, not health targets — use our BMI Calculator alongside this converter for a height-adjusted read on any of these weight figures.

Historical context: same units, opposite starting point

The stone's story is covered in full on our Pounds to Stone calculator page — fixed at 14 pounds avoirdupois by the Weights and Measures Act 1835, with the pound itself later defined in exact metric terms (1lb = 0.45359237kg) through the 1959 International Yard and Pound Agreement. What's worth restating here is why the conversion still matters today: the UK's shift to the metric system from the 1960s onward made kilograms the legal standard for trade, science and medicine, but body weight in everyday British and Irish speech stayed with stone almost universally, leaving two genuinely different but equally "correct" ways of describing the same weight.

That's the specific gap this calculator closes — not a historical curiosity, but a live, everyday translation problem between how people speak (stone) and how records are kept (kilograms).

Where stone to kg conversion actually gets used

  • Medical forms and international travel: visa medicals, international health insurance forms and non-UK medical systems ask for weight in kilograms, requiring a UK stone figure to be converted.
  • Fitness apps and smart scales: many apps and connected scales default to kilograms, prompting UK users who think in stone to convert for a figure they recognise, or vice versa.
  • Sport outside the UK: international competition results, from athletics to combat sports, are reported in kilograms, requiring conversion for UK audiences used to stone commentary.
  • Parcel and luggage limits: some international carriers state weight limits in kilograms, which UK users sometimes want to sanity-check against a stone-based mental estimate.
  • Gym and strength-training programming: weight plates, barbells and strength standards published internationally are almost always in kilograms, requiring UK lifters who think in stone for body weight to convert for programming that references bodyweight percentages.
  • Veterinary and livestock contexts: some UK agricultural and equestrian weight records still use stone (particularly for horses, historically), needing conversion against kilogram-based veterinary dosing guidance.

As with the other conversions in this cluster, the underlying need isn't going away — the UK's mixed use of stone and kilograms for body weight is a stable, long-term feature of everyday British life rather than a temporary transition, so a reliable two-way converter stays useful indefinitely rather than becoming redundant as metrication completes.

Common mistakes when converting stone to kg

  • Skipping the pounds step. Multiplying stone directly by 6.35 without accounting for extra pounds first will silently drop the pounds figure — always convert to total pounds before applying the kilogram factor if there's a pounds component.
  • Confusing decimal stone with stone-and-pounds format. "11.5 stone" is not "11 stone 5lb" — the .5 means half a stone (7lb), so 11.5 stone is actually 11 stone 7lb, not 11 stone 5lb.
  • Rounding the intermediate pounds figure. When converting stone and pounds to kg, keep full precision through the pounds step and round only the final kilogram figure, or the result can drift by a fraction of a kilogram.
  • Assuming "stone" translates internationally. A figure like "11 stone" quoted without context to a non-UK, non-Irish audience will usually need explaining or converting — most of the world has no everyday concept of the stone.

Accuracy and precision note

Because both the stone-to-pounds relationship (exactly 14lb) and the pound-to-kilogram relationship (exactly 0.45359237kg) are fixed, legally defined values, every conversion on this page and in the calculator above is mathematically exact throughout both steps. Kilogram figures are shown to two decimal places for everyday precision; full floating-point precision is available in the calculator if a specific use case needs it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert stone to kg?

Multiply the stone figure by 14 to get pounds, then multiply by 0.45359237 to get kilograms. For example, 11 stone × 14 = 154lb, and 154 × 0.45359237 = 69.85kg.

What is 11 stone in kg?

11 stone is 69.85kg.

What is 11st 11lb in kg?

11 stone 11lb (165lb total) is 74.8kg.

How do I convert kg to stone?

Divide the kilogram figure by 0.45359237 to get pounds, then divide by 14 to get stone. For example, 70kg ÷ 0.45359237 = 154.3lb, and 154.3 ÷ 14 = 11.02 stone, i.e. 11 stone 0.3lb.

What is 10 stone in kg?

10 stone is exactly 63.5kg (140lb × 0.45359237).

What is 14 stone in kg?

14 stone is 88.9kg.

How many kg is one stone?

One stone is approximately 6.35029kg (14 pounds × 0.45359237kg per pound). It isn't a round number in kilograms because the stone and kilogram come from two entirely different measurement systems.

How accurate is a stone to kg conversion?

It's exact, not approximate — both the stone-to-pounds relationship (14lb exactly) and the pound-to-kilogram relationship (0.45359237kg exactly) are fixed, defined values, so a stone-to-kg conversion is precise arithmetic throughout.

What is 9st 7lb in kg?

9 stone 7lb (133lb total) is 60.3kg.

What is 15 stone in kg?

15 stone is 95.3kg.

What is 12st 7lb in kg?

12 stone 7lb (175lb total) is 79.4kg — a frequently quoted average adult reference weight.

A quick note

All conversions above use the exact legal definitions of the stone, pound and kilogram. For anything precision-critical (medical, legal or commercial use), always confirm against the source figures rather than a rounded display value.
