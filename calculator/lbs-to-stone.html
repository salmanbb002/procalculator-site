Pounds to stone: what it means and why the UK still uses it

The stone is the unit Britain still reaches for when talking about a person's body weight, even though the kilogram has been the legal metric standard for most other purposes since the 1960s and 70s. One stone equals exactly 14 pounds (lb), and one pound is defined as exactly 0.45359237 kilograms — both fixed, exact values, so a pounds-to-stone conversion is always precise arithmetic rather than an estimate. The pound itself belongs to the avoirdupois system, the everyday imperial weight system used for general goods (as opposed to troy weight, used historically for precious metals). If you're American, this can be confusing: the US never adopted "stone" as a body-weight unit at all, and simply talks in pounds — so "stone" queries on the web are overwhelmingly a UK and Ireland phenomenon.

This calculator handles both directions people actually search for: converting a raw pounds figure (like 200 pounds in stone) into stone and pounds, and converting a spoken stone-and-pounds figure (like 11 stone 11 pounds) back into a single lb number — useful when you've been given your weight one way and need it in the other for a form, an app, or a conversation.

Conversion formula and method

The formula for pounds to stone is straightforward because both units are fixed and exact:

Stone = Pounds ÷ 14
Remaining pounds = Pounds − (Stone × 14)

Worked example: converting lb to stone and pounds

Take 176 pounds. Divide by 14: 176 ÷ 14 = 12.57. The whole-number part, 12, is the stone figure. To find the leftover pounds, multiply the stone figure back out and subtract: 176 − (12 × 14) = 176 − 168 = 8. So 176lb converts to 12 stone 8lb.

Worked example: converting stone and pounds to lb

Take 13 stone 5lb. Multiply the stone figure by 14, then add the pounds: (13 × 14) + 5 = 182 + 5 = 187lb. This is the calculation the "Stone + lb → Pounds" mode above runs automatically — useful for queries like 8 stone 10 pounds in pounds or 11 stone 11 pounds in lbs.

Converting from kilograms

If you're starting from kilograms rather than pounds, first multiply by 2.20462 to get pounds, then apply the formula above — or use our dedicated KG to Stone calculator, which does both steps in one go and handles decimal kilogram inputs like 63.5kg cleanly.

Body weight in context: real-world reference points

Numbers on their own can be hard to picture, so here's how common weight figures compare to recognisable UK reference points:

  • 10 stone (140lb / 63.5kg) — around the midpoint of the healthy BMI range for an average UK adult height (5'7"–5'9"), and a common target weight in fitness and diet contexts.
  • 12st 7lb (175lb / 79.4kg) — the UK's cruiserweight boxing limit sits close to this mark, and it's a frequently quoted "average adult male" reference in health articles.
  • 14 stone (196lb / 88.9kg) — around the light-heavyweight to cruiserweight boundary in professional boxing, and a common threshold discussed in NHS healthy-weight resources for taller adult men.
  • 8 stone (112lb / 50.8kg) — a typical featherweight boxing limit, and towards the lower end of the healthy weight range for an average adult female height.
  • 20 stone (280lb / 127kg) — a figure that appears often in NHS weight-management and bariatric contexts, roughly double the 10-stone reference point above.

These are illustrative reference points, not health targets — a healthy weight depends on height, frame, muscle mass and individual circumstances. Use our BMI Calculator alongside this converter if you want a height-adjusted read on a specific weight.

Historical context: where the stone comes from

The stone is one of the oldest surviving British weight units, originally an informal measure based on an actual stone used as a counterweight on balance scales — which is exactly why its value varied so much before standardisation. Different trades weighed different commodities in "stones" of different sizes: a stone of wool was traditionally 15lb, a stone of meat or fish was often 8lb, and regional stones for goods like cheese, hemp or glass could differ again. This inconsistency was a genuine problem for trade and taxation.

The Weights and Measures Act 1835 fixed the stone at exactly 14 pounds avoirdupois for general use, bringing legal consistency across England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland. The pound itself, and by extension the stone, was later re-defined in exact metric terms — 1 pound = 0.45359237kg precisely — through international agreement in 1959 (adopted into UK law via instruments including the Weights and Measures Act 1985), which is why today's pounds-to-stone and pounds-to-kilogram conversions are mathematically exact rather than approximate.

The metric system became the UK's primary legal system for trade from the 1960s onward, and by the 1980s most goods sold by weight had to be in metric units. Body weight was one of the few areas metrication never fully displaced — a stone-and-pounds figure remained (and remains) the default way most people in the UK and Ireland describe how much they weigh.

Where you'll still see stone used today

Stone hasn't just survived as a linguistic habit — it shows up in specific, practical UK contexts:

  • Bathroom scales: most scales sold in the UK still offer a stone-and-pounds display mode alongside kilograms, and it remains the default for many households.
  • GP surgeries and the NHS: patients are frequently asked for or told their weight in stone and pounds in everyday conversation, even where the clinical record is kept in kilograms.
  • Combat sports: boxing, wrestling and MMA weight classes are widely discussed in stone and pounds in UK media coverage, even when the official limits are set in kilograms or pounds internationally.
  • Everyday conversation and idiom: "losing a stone", "putting on half a stone over Christmas" and similar phrases are deeply embedded in British English in a way that "losing 6.35 kilograms" simply isn't.
  • Horse racing: jockey and horse handicap weights in British racing are traditionally given in stone and pounds.

Common mistakes when converting lbs to stone

  • Not carrying over a full stone. If your leftover-pounds figure comes out at 14 or more, you've made an arithmetic slip — it should always carry over into an extra stone. For example, 209lb is 14 stone 13lb, not 14 stone 21lb.
  • Confusing decimal stone with stone-and-pounds format. 10.5 stone is not the same as "10 stone 5lb" — the decimal .5 means half a stone, which is 7lb, so 10.5 stone is actually 10 stone 7lb. This mix-up is one of the most common errors in manual conversions.
  • Assuming "stone" means anything outside the UK and Ireland. A figure like "10 stone" quoted without units to an international audience, especially American, will usually be misunderstood — the US has no everyday concept of the stone as a weight unit.
  • Rounding too early in multi-step conversions. If you're going from kilograms to stone via pounds, round only the final answer, not the intermediate pounds figure — rounding early can shift the final stone-and-pounds result by a pound or more at larger weights.

Accuracy and precision note

Because the pound and the stone are both fixed, exact definitions (1lb = 0.45359237kg exactly; 1 stone = 14lb exactly), every conversion in this calculator and on this page is mathematically exact — there's no approximation involved in the underlying maths. The only rounding happens in how results are displayed: kilogram figures are shown to one decimal place for readability, and decimal-stone figures to two decimal places. If you need a raw, unrounded figure for a specific use case, the whole-pounds and whole-stone-plus-pounds figures shown are exact and can be used directly.

Frequently asked questions

How many pounds are in a stone?

There are exactly 14 pounds in a stone. This has been the legal UK definition since the Weights and Measures Act 1835, and it applies consistently whether you're weighing a person, a bag of potatoes, or anything else measured in stone.

How do I convert pounds to stone?

Divide the number of pounds by 14. The whole number part of the result is the stone, and multiplying the remaining decimal by 14 gives the leftover pounds. For example, 170 ÷ 14 = 12.14, so 170lbs is 12 stone plus 0.14 × 14 ≈ 2lb, i.e. 12 stone 2lb.

What is 200 pounds in stone?

200 pounds is 14 stone 4lb, or 14.29 stone as a decimal. In kilograms, that's approximately 90.7kg.

How do I convert stone and pounds back into pounds?

Multiply the stone figure by 14, then add the extra pounds. For example, 11 stone 11lb converts to (11 × 14) + 11 = 165lb. The calculator on this page does this instantly using the "Stone + lb → Pounds" mode.

What is 150lbs in stone and pounds?

150lbs is 10 stone 10lb. As a decimal, that's 10.71 stone, and in kilograms it's roughly 68kg.

Is the stone used outside the UK and Ireland?

No. The stone as a body-weight unit is essentially unique to the UK and Ireland today. The United States uses pounds directly, and most of the rest of the world uses kilograms, so "stone" rarely appears outside British and Irish contexts.

Why does the UK still measure body weight in stone?

It's largely cultural continuity — bathroom scales, GP surgery conversations, boxing and wrestling weigh-ins, and everyday speech in the UK have kept "stone" as the default way people describe body weight, even though the metric kilogram is the legal unit for trade and most other measurements.

Is a stone always exactly 14 pounds, or has it changed?

Today it's always 14lb, fixed by the Weights and Measures Act 1835 and reaffirmed in later legislation including the Weights and Measures Act 1985. Historically, before standardisation, the stone varied by commodity and region — a stone of wool, for instance, was traditionally 15lb rather than 14lb.

How accurate is a pounds-to-stone conversion?

It's exact, not approximate — a stone is defined as precisely 14 pounds, so dividing by 14 gives a mathematically exact answer. Any rounding you see is just in how many decimal places are displayed, not in the underlying conversion.

What is 220lbs in stone?

220lbs converts to 15 stone 10lb, or 15.71 stone as a decimal — around 99.8kg.

A quick note

All conversions above use the exact legal definitions of the pound and stone. For anything precision-critical (medical, legal or commercial use), always confirm against the source figures rather than a rounded display value.
